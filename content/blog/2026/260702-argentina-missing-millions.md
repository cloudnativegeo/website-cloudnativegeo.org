*Nissim Lebovits is a technical fellow at Radiant Earth and a former Fulbright research fellow in La Plata, Argentina.*

Argentina's official census is probably missing roughly [3.4 million people](https://barriosvisibles.org). All it takes to prove this is open building footprints and a simple spatial join. This isn't an indictment of the folks behind the Argentine census; rather, it's a demonstration of the rapidly growing power of [cloud-native geo (CNG)](https://cloudnativegeo.org/), and why the next frontier for our community is turning our attention to an audience we've never reached before.

I spent most of 2025 in Argentina on a Fulbright fellowship. My master's is in city planning, and I was in grad school when global datasets like [open building footprints](https://source.coop/vida/google-microsoft-open-buildings) were starting to proliferate. It was easy for me to see what these data could offer, especially for small cities in places that have not historically had access to data of that caliber. With that in mind, I spent my time in Argentina working with four municipal governments to understand how they could take advantage of these new datasets for climate risk planning. We worked on issues like flooding and extreme heat, using data like [JRC's global riverine flood hazard](https://source.coop/nlebovits/jrc-glofas) and [Landsat LST composites](https://www.usgs.gov/landsat-missions/landsat-collection-2-surface-temperature) and incorporating our results [into actual municipal plans](https://www.laopinionline.ar/Compromiso-de-Pergamino-con-el-cambio-climatico-y-las-olas-de-calor). My goal was not only to explore what was possible with these global datasets, but to understand from a user's perspective what the opportunities and obstacles to using them were.

While in Argentina, I was based in the city of La Plata (official pop. ~800,000), the capital of Buenos Aires Province. La Plata has a long history of flooding, and in April of 2013, they suffered a catastrophic flood that led to more than 80 deaths.

{{< img src="images/260702-la-plata-floods-2013.jpg" alt="NASA's Terra spacecraft satellite view of severe flooding in La Plata, Argentina, in April 2013" attr="Image: NASA" attrlink="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:NASA_Spacecraft_Eyes_Severe_Flooding_in_Argentina.jpg" >}}

The 2013 flood marked a turning point for the city, though, and led to the establishment of an ongoing agreement between the municipal government and the [National University of La Plata](https://unlp.edu.ar/) (aka the UNLP, my host institution) to collaborate on flood risk analyses and policymaking. This included, for instance, flood hazard maps generated by the engineering department, and risk assessments produced by the department of city planning, where I was doing my research. Because of this agreement, La Plata is fairly data-rich by the standards of most Argentine cities, and, in fact, Argentina itself has a robust open geo data community, spearheaded by the [Infraestructura de Datos Espaciales de la República Argentina (IDERA)](https://www.idera.gob.ar/), which organizes an annual conference and supports hundreds of WFS and WMS endpoints from national, regional, and local governments, as well as various universities. And yet, as I found, La Plata still has a ton to gain from cloud-native geo.

While I was at the UNLP, I was asked to help update the city's official maps of flood risk in informal settlements. In the past, these estimates were based on [RENABAP](https://argentina.gob.ar/habitat/renabap), which is Argentina's official register of informal settlements. Per RENABAP, there are roughly [112,000 residents](https://nlebovits.github.io/ciut-riesgo/renabap.html) in La Plata's informal settlements, spread across 166 neighborhoods. However, RENABAP (and Argentina's official census more generally) is notoriously limited. There are several reasons for this. First, conducting a national census is just really hard, even in the [world's wealthiest countries](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2022/06/08/key-facts-about-the-quality-of-the-2020-census/). Moreover, informal settlements are hard to access, often suspicious of government agencies, and, in many cases, grow rapidly. RENABAP's data were collected between 2017 and 2023, for instance, a period that saw both the COVID-19 pandemic and a program of stark austerity under the Milei government, under which rates of unemployment, poverty, and inflation have been in constant flux ([El País](https://english.elpais.com/international/2026-04-12/argentinas-milei-is-struggling-with-the-economy-and-losing-popularity.html)). On top of all of that are the political considerations of who does and doesn't want these data published.[^1] But, even in light of all of that, RENABAP offers the best available data on informal settlements in Argentina, and it's what I was asked to use in my analysis.

In previous years, the [CIUT](https://ciut.fau.unlp.edu.ar) (my research institute within the UNLP) had estimated flood risk by taking the percent overlap of the flood hazard zone with each informal settlement, and multiplying that ratio by RENABAP's estimated population in the settlement. I'd read [studies](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-09282-y) showing that this kind of area-weighted interpolation can lead to imprecise estimates of flood risk, so my original goal was simply to supplement the RENABAP data with the Google-Microsoft-OSM open building footprints data hosted on [Source Cooperative](https://source.coop). I thought this might give me a better understanding of the spatial distribution of population, but instead I found that it kept giving me population estimates that diverged wildly from RENABAP's numbers. At first, I was convinced I'd managed to completely screw up a simple spatial join, but as I dug into my code and explored the actual data in QGIS, I realized that there was no way around it: there were just way more people in these settlements than the official numbers accounted for. One particularly egregious case was a settlement called Los Hornos, where RENABAP reported 330 families, but I saw more than 4,200 building footprints:

{{< img src="images/260702-los-hornos-satellite.png" alt="Satellite imagery of the Los Hornos settlement with building footprints overlaid, far outnumbering the RENABAP family count" >}}

In settlement after settlement, I found that footprints outnumbered the RENABAP estimates by an average of more than 40%. Across all of La Plata, this translated to 72,000 building footprints, relative to an estimated 34,000 families (per RENABAP). Because informal settlements tend to have single-story, single-family buildings; little to no non-residential use; and near-100% occupancy, this means that these footprints are a strong proxy for actual population; in my working paper, I estimate that, based on the number of building footprints, there are approximately 221,000 to 265,000 people in La Plata's informal settlements — roughly 150,000 more than the official estimate. A separate, independent effort from the Universidad Católica de La Plata points in the same direction, putting La Plata's informal-settlement population at roughly 260,000 — also well above the official count.[^2]

If there are 150,000 people unaccounted for in La Plata — a city that is supposedly home to only 800,000 people — what does this analysis look like nationally? This was my immediate question after finishing up in La Plata. And, as it turns out, when this approach is run across all of Argentina, it shows that there are about 3.4 million people missing from Argentina's national record of informal settlements (as well as the official census), which, in a country of ~46 million represents something like 7% of the population.

Here's how those numbers shake out. Nationally, the combined Google-Microsoft-OSM dataset turns up 1.97 million building footprints inside RENABAP's 6,467 registered barrios — 59% more than the 1.24 million families on record. Once I apply RENABAP's own ratio of roughly 1.1 families per dwelling and some deliberately conservative people-per-family multipliers, that works out to somewhere between 6.3 and 7.6 million people living in Argentina's informal settlements, or 2.9 to 3.4 million more than the official figure. The one place the footprints actually *undercount* is the City of Buenos Aires, where the consolidated vertical *villas* stack multiple families under a single roofline; but everywhere else — and especially in the urban periphery — building counts run well ahead of the family estimates, exactly the kind of systematic undercount that the international literature would lead you to expect.

The implications of these findings are massive for Argentina, of course — but even more important is the fact that CNG tooling makes this same analysis possible for *any other country in the world.* Anyone with access to the internet, a regular laptop, and a rough idea of where an informal settlement is can quickly and reliably estimate its population. And it's not only informal settlements that this applies to; CNG transforms what is possible for low-capacity actors like Global South governments. This is true for three reasons.

First, CNG makes high-quality global datasets accessible. Downloading a GeoJSON of the entire global building footprints dataset (or even a country-level version, for that matter) is unwieldy at best and almost certainly impossible for a municipal GIS analyst running on the world's slowest internet somewhere in Córdoba Province, Argentina. But if she can just download a handful of bytes corresponding to, say, the City of Esperanza, using the data suddenly becomes viable. This is particularly important because we're now in a moment where global data often outperform local data. With institutions like [Overture](https://overturemaps.org/) producing high-quality global data and object storage approaching near-zero marginal cost to redistribute, many global datasets now offer far better data — far more accessibly — than a small municipality would ever be able to produce on its own.

{{< img src="images/260702-source-coop.png" alt="The Google-Microsoft-OSM Open Buildings dataset page on Source Cooperative, showing 2.7 billion footprints available as GeoParquet, FlatGeobuf, and PMTiles" >}}

Second, CNG enables continental-scale analyses. My entire workflow here was a spatial join — I'm talking GIS 101 stuff. But it's a large calculation, because it's 33.8 million building footprints being joined to 6,467 informal settlement polygons across more than 2.7 million square kilometers. That is not an easy analysis to run with legacy geospatial tools — and I know this because a colleague at the Uruguayan National Statistical Institute (INE) tried to replicate my same analysis for Uruguay, a country which is roughly 6% the total area of Argentina, and gave up after two hours because PostGIS just couldn't get it done. On the other hand, thanks to optimized [GeoParquet](https://geoparquet.org) and [DuckDB](https://duckdb.org), I was able to run the entire analysis for Argentina on my laptop in 3.1 seconds.

{{< img src="images/260702-spatial-join.png" alt="The full Argentina spatial join running in DuckDB in 3.1 seconds" >}}

Finally, CNG makes these analyses *trustworthy*. I was worried about sharing these findings; claiming that 3.4 million people are missing in a national census is kind of a big deal. My first move was to publish a working paper on my website, with reproducible code, sensitivity analyses, etc. But then I realized: if you look at the settlement boundaries and building footprints overlaid on a satellite basemap, you can just count the building footprints yourself. So I spun up [barriosvisibles.org](https://barriosvisibles.org) to show every settlement and building footprint in Argentina, overlaid on top of a recent satellite basemap, and I built the whole map in an afternoon with nothing but [PMTiles](https://protomaps.com/docs/pmtiles) in the browser. Because CNG makes it so easy to share these data, an elected official in a risk-averse local government doesn't have to slog through sensitivity analyses in a working paper; they just have to look at the map.

{{< img src="images/260702-barrios-visibles-zoom.gif" alt="Zooming in on barriosvisibles.org to see individual building footprints over satellite imagery" >}}

In Argentina, everyone I showed these data to immediately understood the potential impact. I didn't have to try hard to convince local officials that this is something worth spending time on; they all got it, and they repeatedly told me that the main obstacle for them was simply not knowing the data were out there to begin with. This is exciting to me because it means the next step is not about more *tech*, but more *awareness*.

So we've reached an inflection point in the CNG community. The data and tooling that we've spent years developing for high-capacity users like NASA and ESA can now also be applied in low-capacity contexts, like municipal governments in the Global South. And we're sitting on an enormous opportunity for impact; if 3.4 million people are missing in Argentina — a relatively data-rich country — what do those numbers look like in El Salvador, Malawi, or Pakistan? The work now is not about building more pipelines or APIs or standards, but building awareness in the communities that have the most to gain from what we've already built.
